FAQ

What do "Specific", "weak hits", "possible off-target" and "likely off-target" mean?

Primer-BLAST searches the whole human genome for every other place where the two primers (or one primer twice) could bind close enough to each other to make a PCR product, even with mismatches. It lists each of those products under the pair. PrimerScope then grades each listed product by how well the primers match it, especially at the 3′ end (the last 5 bases). The 3′ end is where the polymerase starts copying. One or two mismatches there usually stop the extension, while mismatches near the 5′ end matter much less.

LabelRule used for each extra productWhat it means at the bench
SpecificPrimer-BLAST found no other product.Best case.
Specific · N weak hitsEvery extra product has at least one primer with ≥2 mismatches in its last 5 bases (NCBI's default rule), or too many mismatches overall, or is too long to amplify in a standard PCR.Effectively specific. Weak hits almost never amplify.
N possible off-targetsBoth primers have ≤1 mismatch in their last 5 bases, ≤5 mismatches in total, and the product is ≤1,500 bp.May give a faint extra band or a little background in the trace. Usually acceptable. Check the gel, or pick another pair if you have one.
N likely off-targetsBoth primers have ≤1 mismatch in their last 5 bases, ≤3 mismatches in total, and the product is ≤4,000 bp.Expect co-amplification, typically a pseudogene or paralog. Mixed Sanger traces are likely. Avoid this pair.

So "3 possible off-targets" means three other genomic sites could give a product, none of them a likely one. Open the pair to see each site, its product size and the mismatches per primer. A product of a very different size from yours can be seen on a gel and cut out, but anything amplified alongside your product is also sequenced by the same primer.

Primer-BLAST or Local Primer3: which one should I pick?

Both tabs use the same engine (Primer3) with the same settings: product size, Tm, GC, margin and gnomAD exclusions. They differ in what they can see.

  • Local Primer3 runs instantly in your browser, on the fetched template around the variant only. It checks primer chemistry (Tm, hairpins, dimers), common SNPs, repeats and similarity to known human repeat families. It cannot see the rest of the genome, so it cannot detect a pseudogene or paralog.
  • Primer-BLAST (NCBI) designs candidates the same way, then BLASTs each one against the human genome and only returns pairs that pass its specificity screen. It takes 1–6 minutes and depends on NCBI being up.

Rule of thumb: order a Primer-BLAST pair labelled Specific or Specific · weak hits with no red notes. Use a local pair only if Primer-BLAST fails or has no good pair, and then press Check specificity on that pair (it runs Primer-BLAST on those exact primers) or open UCSC In-Silico PCR before ordering. When a local pair is also in the Primer-BLAST list, its row shows the Primer-BLAST result automatically. For genes with a known pseudogene, which get a warning at the top, always rely on Primer-BLAST.

How do I read the Locus map? Can I zoom in?

The map is the DNA around your variant, drawn in the gene's orientation: the gene runs left (5′) to right (3′). For a minus-strand gene this is the reverse complement of the chromosome. The numbers on top are distances in bp from the variant (0).

  • Orange bar: the variant. Pale orange band: the margin, where no primer may sit.
  • EXONS: grey blocks are exons of the transcript (ex6 = exon 6). The dashed line is intron.
  • REPEATS: hatched blocks are repeats (Alu, LINE, simple repeats…) from Ensembl. Primers there tend to bind in many places.
  • SEG. DUP: purple blocks are segmental duplications, stretches of at least 1 kb with a ≥90% identical copy elsewhere in the genome (UCSC). Darker means more identical. Hover to see where the copy is.
  • SNPS: each tick is a population variant from gnomAD v4 (or dbSNP common variants if gnomAD is down). Taller ticks are more frequent: the scale is logarithmic, from 0.01% to 100%, using the highest population frequency. Orange ticks are at or above your threshold, and primers are kept off them to avoid allele dropout.
  • PRIMERS: the selected pair. The green arrow is the forward primer and the teal arrow the reverse one. The dashed line between them is the product, labelled with its length.
  • READS: what each Sanger read will show. The faded first ~40 bases of each read are normally unreadable, and the variant should sit in the solid part of both reads.

Zoom with the buttons above the map. Whole template shows everything that was fetched. Amplicon fills the width with the selected product. Variant (bases) shows the variant and its margin base by base, with the reference letters. Hover any element for details. The annotated amplicon sequence under the selected pair is the full base-level view of the product.

Can I choose how long the amplicon (PCR product) will be?

Yes. Set Amplicon size Min / Max under the variant box, or click a preset. Primer3 and Primer-BLAST only return pairs whose product falls inside that range. The exact length of each pair is in the Product bp column, on the map and in the pair details.

  • Standard 300–650 bp (default) suits most Sanger runs. A read gives roughly 700–900 good bases after a ~40 base unreadable start, so both reads cover the whole product.
  • Short 200–400 bp suits degraded DNA (FFPE) or a GC-rich region that amplifies poorly.
  • Long 600–900 bp lets one amplicon cover a whole exon or two nearby variants. The middle may then be read well by only one of the two reads.
  • To aim for one length, use a narrow range such as 480–520. Expect fewer pairs, and sometimes none.
  • The shortest possible product is about 2 × margin + 2 × primer length + the variant length, e.g. 2×60 + 2×20 + 1 ≈ 160 bp with the defaults.
What does the "Segmental duplication" warning mean?

Part of the DNA around your variant has a near-identical copy somewhere else in the genome, usually a pseudogene or a paralog. The data come from the UCSC segmental duplication track, which covers every gene, so the warning no longer depends on a fixed list of known genes. Primers that sit in the duplicated stretch can amplify both copies, and the Sanger trace then shows the two copies mixed, which can look like a heterozygous variant.

  • Prefer a Primer-BLAST pair marked Specific. Primer-BLAST checks the copy directly.
  • A local Primer3 primer inside a duplication gets a note on its row. Press Check specificity before ordering it.
  • At ≥98% identity (red), the copies may be impossible to tell apart with short primers. The usual fix is a long, copy-specific PCR first, then sequencing from inside it.
What does the VariantValidator pill on the variant card mean?

For variants typed in c. notation, PrimerScope asks VariantValidator whether the description is valid on that transcript, while the design runs. This catches numbering mistakes before you order primers for the wrong position. It does not judge the variant itself.

  • VariantValidator ✓: the description is valid as typed.
  • VariantValidator: c.…: it is valid, but VariantValidator writes it differently (e.g. shifted 3′ in a repeat). Hover to see the warnings.
  • VariantValidator ✗ or other position: the description is invalid, or VariantValidator maps it somewhere else. Check the transcript and numbering.

Genomic, VCF-style and rs inputs are not sent, because they already name a genomic position.

What happens when gnomAD or Ensembl is down?

PrimerScope falls back to the UCSC Genome Browser. RepeatMasker from UCSC replaces the Ensembl repeats. dbSNP 155 common variants replace gnomAD. That fallback only lists variants with a frequency of at least 1% in at least one population project, so rarer variants above your threshold are not screened, and a message says so. If both sources fail, the SNP step shows "Unavailable".

What do "Variant at base F / R" and the margin mean?

They give the position of the variant counted from the 3′ end of each primer, i.e. how far into each Sanger read the variant appears. The first 30–50 bases of a read are usually unreadable, so the margin (Design settings, default 60 bp) keeps both primers at least that far away. A variant beyond ~650 bases is near the end of a typical good read and is flagged.

Why does it avoid gnomAD variants under the primers?

If a common variant sits under a primer, especially in its last 5 bases, the primer can fail to bind the allele that carries it. That allele then drops out of the PCR. A heterozygous patient would look homozygous and the variant could be missed. Primers are kept off variants whose highest population frequency is at or above your threshold (default 0.1%). Variants below it are listed under the pair for information.

What do the notes on a pair mean (hairpin, dimer, repeat consensus)?
  • Hairpin / self-dimer / pair dimer Tm: the temperature at which a primer folds on itself or pairs with the other primer. Above 47 °C (Primer3's limit) it can compete with real binding or form primer-dimers.
  • Resembles a repeat consensus: the local primer looks like a human repeat family, so it may bind many places. Pairs screened by Primer-BLAST do not need this check.
  • Matches more than once inside the template: the primer occurs twice in the fetched template.
Why did it stop with "Reference mismatch"?

The reference base you typed (the C in c.652C>T) is not what the genome has at that position. This usually means another transcript or version, or a numbering error. Check the transcript, or press Design anyway if you are sure.

How can I double-check a pair outside PrimerScope?
  • UCSC In-Silico PCR (button on the pair) runs both primers against hg38 and lists every product. It is a fast second opinion on specificity.
  • UCSC browser and gnomAD region open the amplicon so you can inspect SNPs, repeats and segmental duplications.
  • Open in Primer-BLAST reopens the exact job on NCBI's own page.

Designs are predictions. Confirm each pair with a test PCR and one sequencing run before diagnostic use.
